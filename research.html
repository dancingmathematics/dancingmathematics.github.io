Research

Research & projects

Design-based research at the intersection of embodied cognition, mathematics education, and cultural practice — conducted in Berkeley, in Bali, and in collaboration with colleagues across Europe, Asia, and North America.

Approach

Most mathematics is taught as though the body were incidental to it. My work starts from the opposite premise: that perception, gesture, and coordinated movement are not aids to mathematical reasoning but part of its material. What follows from that premise is a design question — if reasoning is embodied, what should a learning environment be made of?

I answer that question through embodied design: building activities and artefacts that make invisible perceptual structures available for inspection, then studying what learners actually do with them. The empirical work uses multimodal interaction analysis, microgenetic study of individual learning episodes, and, increasingly, mobile eye tracking and motion capture.

The cultural dimension is not decoration. Movement practices such as Balinese dance already contain sophisticated spatial reasoning, developed and transmitted outside of formal mathematics. Treating them as genuine epistemic resources — rather than as a motivational wrapper around Western content — is the commitment that organises the rest of my research.

Flagship · lead researcher 2023–present

Geometry Resources in Dance (GRiD)

Auxiliary line construction is essential to geometry problem-solving and comes naturally to almost no one. GRiD begins from an observation I made about my own practice: the imaginary reference lines I use spontaneously when dancing are structurally the same objects as the auxiliary lines a student is asked to draw. The design is a low-cost gridded floor mat, plus markers, yarn, straws, and elastic bands, on which children perform Balinese postures, evaluate their own accuracy, and use the grid to justify their positioning geometrically. Studies to date involve fifth-grade students in Bali and in California with varying dance experience.

Principal investigator 2024–present

Digital GRiD: real-time movement tracing for embodied geometry learning

A webcam-based movement tracer that gives learners real-time visual feedback during embodied geometry activity, developed with Yangyang Yang (UC Berkeley). Colour detection renders the path of a moving limb as a visible trace, letting children discover, for instance, that a circle is what a fixed radius leaves behind.

Funding: ICBS Interdisciplinary Grant, Institute of Cognitive and Brain Sciences, UC Berkeley (2026, $4,000); Innovation Catalyst Grant, Jacobs Institute for Design Innovation, UC Berkeley (2024, $500).

Principal investigator International

GRiD with real-time mobile eye tracking

Two learners standing on opposite sides of the same mat do not see the same figure. This strand investigates heteroperception — the breakdown and reconciliation of collaborating learners’ differing views of a shared mathematical object — through multimodal interaction analysis and mobile eye tracking.

Collaborators: Dr. Catalina Lomos (Luxembourg Institute of Socio-Economic Research), Assistant Professor Alik Palatnik (The Hebrew University of Jerusalem), Professor Dor Abrahamson (UC Berkeley). Supported by the Luxembourg National Research Fund (FNR), 2025.

Collaborative 2025–present

Dance–mathematics phenomenological study

How do dancers experience, articulate, and coordinate mathematical and aesthetic relations through movement? A self-funded international collaboration approaching the question from the dancer’s side rather than the classroom’s.

With Dr. Anna Shvarts (Utrecht University), Dr. Shabari Rao (National Institute of Advanced Studies), Dr. Janani Suresh Ram (Germany), Francesca Neri Macchiaverna (Università Roma Tre), and Dr. Eka Zharinova (United Kingdom).

Project lead 2026–present

Dance as a resource in mathematics education

An international collaborative inquiry into how dance practices — plural, and from several traditions — can function as epistemic and pedagogical resources in mathematics education rather than as illustrations of it.

With Dafna Efron (The Hebrew University of Jerusalem), Dr. Shabari Rao (National Institute of Advanced Studies), and Shanti Pise (University of Hertfordshire).

Project lead 2026–present

Funds of knowledge of transnational mathematics teacher educators

A collaborative autoethnography, built from iterative narrative-reflective dialogue, examining how transnational mathematics teacher educators reconstruct professional knowledge as they move across educational contexts — and what gets lost, translated, or quietly abandoned in the process.

With Dr. Angga Hidayat (Florida Gulf Coast University), Ahmad Wachidul Kohar (Michigan State University), Anis Munfarikhatin (Syracuse University), Dayat Hidayat (Purdue University), Said Fachry Assagaf (Indiana University Bloomington), and Sadrack Pagiling (Michigan State University).
