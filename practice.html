How I teach

I began teaching mathematics content courses — calculus, linear algebra, abstract algebra, complex analysis — and moved gradually toward courses about learning itself. What carried across was a conviction that students should be given problems whose structure they can feel before they can name, and time enough to notice what they did.

In the CalTeach programme at Berkeley I work with prospective science and mathematics teachers on lesson planning, classroom observation, analysis of student thinking, and equity-oriented pedagogy. Much of the value there is in slowing down: watching a video of a child reasoning until the class stops evaluating her and starts understanding her.

Practice

Balinese dance

My education in Balinese dance has been rooted primarily in community participation. I learned by observing, rehearsing, and performing with others at community events and religious ceremonies. I performed my first dance when I was five years old. Two or three years later, I began studying at Sanggar Tari Warini under the Balinese dance maestro Ni Ketut Arini. Although my formal academic education eventually led me into mathematics education, dance kept returning throughout my life. I danced with my department’s performance group as an undergraduate, found opportunities to dance again while pursuing my master’s degree in the Netherlands, and continued dancing during my doctoral studies in Berkeley, California. Since 2021, I have performed at more than thirty public events across Northern California, occasionally at the invitation of the Consulate General of the Republic of Indonesia in San Francisco.

Why this is on a research website

Balinese dance is a discipline of exact spatial relations. Agem requires the body to hold a specific configuration of angles. Tapak Sirang Pada asks the feet to form ninety degrees. Travelling across a stage means maintaining a relation to a reference you cannot see and no one has drawn for you.

Dancers do this by constructing lines — between the shoulder and the far edge of the stage, between one foot and a fixed point behind, between the body and the audience. These lines are imagined, held briefly, and discarded. Nobody teaches them explicitly and nobody writes them down.

They are also, structurally, auxiliary lines: the same objects that make geometry problems solvable. Recognising that correspondence is what the GRiD project is built on. So the performance record below is not a hobby appended to a CV. It is the empirical ground the research rests on, and I keep it with the same care.
