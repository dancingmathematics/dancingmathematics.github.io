Geometry Resources in Dance

A gridded floor mat on which the imaginary reference lines of Balinese dance become the auxiliary lines of geometry — and children discover they already know how to think with them.

Rationale

Constructing an auxiliary line — the extra line you draw that was not in the original figure — is one of the most powerful moves in geometry and one of the least teachable. Students can be shown a hundred worked examples and still not know where the line should go, because the difficulty is not procedural. The line has to occur to you.

The question that started this project was therefore not “how do we teach auxiliary lines?” but: what would be a situation where the lines come intuitively as one’s strategy to solve a problem?

My answer came from outside mathematics education. Trained both as a mathematics educator and as a Balinese dancer, I noticed that the imaginary reference lines I use spontaneously while dancing — to check whether my foot is placed correctly, whether my shoulders are square to the audience, whether I have travelled the right distance across the floor — do exactly the work that auxiliary lines do in a geometric proof. They are invisible, constructed on demand, and used to make a spatial relation checkable.

These are what the embodied cognition literature calls attentional anchors: perceptual structures a person creates, usually without awareness, to stabilise a difficult coordination. In dance they stay tacit. GRiD is an attempt to give them somewhere to become explicit.

Design framework

GRiD follows an embodied design methodology: rather than explaining a concept and then asking students to apply it, the design creates a movement task whose successful performance already requires the target structure, and then supplies materials that let students objectify what they did.

The physical kit is deliberately low-cost and low-tech, so that it can be reproduced in a classroom in Bali as easily as in a laboratory in Berkeley:

  • a gridded floor mat large enough to dance on
  • markers and stickers for fixing positions
  • coloured yarn and pipe cleaners for drawing lines in space
  • straws and elastic bands for making angles and rigid segments

Each of these is a way of turning a perceptual construction into an object that can be pointed at, disagreed about, and revised — which is what makes it available for mathematical discourse.

Activities & a sample problem

Students work on what we call dancing-geometry problems. A typical sequence runs:

  1. Perform. Take a Balinese posture — for example Tapak Sirang Pada, in which the feet form a ninety-degree configuration.
  2. Evaluate. Decide whether the posture is accurate, and say how you know.
  3. Justify. Use the grid, the yarn, and the straws to give a geometric account of the position: which lines are perpendicular, which distances are equal, which angle is being claimed.
  4. Relocate. Move the formation to a different part of the mat while preserving the geometric relationships — the step that forces the relation, rather than the location, to become the object of attention.
Why the fourth step matters. As long as a student is standing on square C4, “correct” can mean “in the place the teacher showed me.” Ask them to do the same thing three metres to the left and correctness has to be re-derived from the relations themselves. This is the moment where the auxiliary line stops being decoration and starts being an argument.

Research participants

Studies to date have involved fifth-grade students, ages nine to thirteen, in both Bali, Indonesia and California, with a deliberately wide range of prior Balinese dance experience — from trained young dancers to children encountering the movement vocabulary for the first time. Comparing across that range is how the project distinguishes what depends on cultural fluency from what the design itself can occasion.

Digital GRiD

Beginning in 2024, in collaboration with Yangyang Yang (UC Berkeley), the project developed a real-time movement tracer: webcam-based colour detection that draws the path of a moving limb on screen as it moves. A child sweeping an arm at a fixed distance from a fixed point sees a circle appear — not as a definition to be memorised, but as the residue of something she just did with her body.

The digital layer does not replace the mat. It adds a second inscription surface, and lets us ask what changes when the trace is produced by the system rather than laid down by hand in yarn.

Workshops & international collaboration

  • 2024

    EDaMMLA — Embodied Design and Multimodal Learning Analytics

    UC Berkeley, May 2024

    An international workshop on multimodal learning analytics, with Balinese dancer I Made “Cat” Suteja, featuring motion-capture data collection and coloured-yarn arrangements of dancers’ spatial relations. Chaired by Dor Abrahamson and Alexander Refsum Jensenius.

  • 2024–25

    Copernicus Science Center, Warsaw

    Warsaw, Poland

    Presentations and co-design sessions with Polish colleagues at the Tinkatorium Station, adapting the GRiD approach to Polish cultural movement contexts — the first test of whether the framework travels beyond the tradition it came from.

  • Apr 2025

    Expanding Your Horizons at Berkeley

    UC Berkeley

    “Dancing geometry” workshop for fifth- to eighth-grade girls, trans youth, and gender non-conforming and non-binary students, with Ilona J. Surjorahardjo and Katie Wirjadi.

  • Jul 2025

    Bridges: Mathematics and Art — Family Day

    Eindhoven, the Netherlands

    Facilitated the “Dancing geometry” family activity in the Dancing and Mathematics programme coordinated by Karl Schaffer.
